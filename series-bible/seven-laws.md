# Memory of Stars — The Seven Laws

**Version:** 0.1 · **Status:** Canonical · **Role:** The constraints every story must obey.

> Most fictional universes make the mistake of defining **technology**. We define
> **constraints**. Constraints create stories. Technology solves them.
>
> These borrow from Asimov's Three Laws—not because they're about robots, but because
> they're *simple enough that every writer can remember them*. If someone can't memorize
> the rules, there are too many.
>
> Companion document: [The Universe Foundation](foundation.md).

---

These are not physical laws.

These are narrative laws.

Every story should feel like it naturally exists inside them.

---

## Law 1 — Distance Is Real

> **The stars are far apart.**

Travel is possible.

It is never free.

Time, energy, risk, or opportunity must always be sacrificed.

No civilization can govern the galaxy in real time.

Isolation creates culture.

Culture creates diversity.

**Why:** This keeps exploration meaningful. It prevents Star Trek transporter syndrome. The frontier always exists.

---

## Law 2 — Memory Outlives Flesh

> **Everything leaves memories behind.**

People. Ships. Cities. Wars. Civilizations.

Sometimes those memories are biological.

Sometimes digital.

Sometimes cultural.

Sometimes architectural.

Memory outlives the body.

But it is not immortal, and it is not safe.

What is remembered endures.

What no one holds is lost.

The question is whether anyone can still interpret what remains — and whether anyone still keeps it at all.

**Why:** This is the central mechanic of the universe. Archaeology. Religion. Politics. Identity. Everything flows from this. To be forgotten is not to fade — it is to be lost. So remembering is never passive: it is what keeps the past alive.

---

## Law 3 — Intelligence Is Not Life

> **Consciousness comes in many forms.**

Humans. Animals. Artificial minds. Planetary intelligences. Living ecosystems. Ancient machine civilizations.

None are inherently superior.

Every form of intelligence experiences reality differently.

The stories never assume humanity is the endpoint of evolution.

**Why:** No evil AI clichés. No magical human exceptionalism.

---

## Law 4 — Technology Changes Culture

> **Every invention creates a new civilization.**

FTL doesn't just change transportation.

It changes economics. Families. Religion. Language. Government.

Every important technology should permanently reshape society.

If it doesn't... it isn't important enough.

**Why:** This keeps the world believable.

---

## Law 5 — Every Civilization Forgets Something

> **Memory is always incomplete.**

No society remembers everything.

Every civilization edits its own past.

Sometimes intentionally.

Sometimes accidentally.

Sometimes because history became myth.

Truth is always recoverable. Never obvious.

**Why:** This creates endless stories. Every mystery starts here.

---

## Law 6 — There Are No Chosen Ones

> **History emerges from relationships, not destiny.**

Nobody is born special.

Nobody fulfills prophecy.

Nobody is secretly royalty.

Great people change history because of their choices. Not because the universe selected them.

**Why:** Readers should think: "I could have made that decision."

---

## Law 7 — Wonder Comes First

> **Every story must contain one moment that reminds us how small we are.**

Not horror. Wonder.

Seeing something older than humanity.

Crossing a nebula.

Standing inside a Dyson shell.

Discovering a library the size of a moon.

Listening to a civilization speak after a million years of silence.

Every story should have one scene where the reader simply whispers... "Wow."

---

## Things That Are Intentionally Undefined

This is just as important. We refuse to answer these questions until stories demand answers.

* How does FTL actually work?
* Who built the oldest megastructures?
* Are the "gods" machines, aliens, humans, or something else?
* Is Earth still alive?
* Is humanity alone?
* Can consciousness survive death?
* What happened before recorded history?
* Is time linear?
* Are memories objective?

The biggest mistake science fiction makes is answering these too early. Every answer closes doors.

> When a story finally answers one of these, it is a **universe-level canon event** —
> log it in [`canon-and-continuity.md`](canon-and-continuity.md) so it stays answered
> the same way everywhere afterward.

---

## The Writer's Test

Before any novella is accepted, ask:

- [ ] Does it teach the reader one new truth about the universe?
- [ ] Does it leave one important mystery unsolved?
- [ ] Does at least one character permanently change because of the events?
- [ ] Does civilization itself change, even in a small way?
- [ ] Does the ending make the universe feel larger than it did at the beginning?

If not... rewrite it.

---

## The Organizing Metaphor — Civilization Is a Conversation Across Generations

Every great fictional universe has an organizing metaphor.

* **The Force** is about balance and faith.
* **The One Ring** is about the corrupting nature of power.
* **The Spice** is about dependency, foresight, and control.

For *Memory of Stars*:

> **Civilization is a conversation across generations.**

Memory isn't just storage. It's the act of one generation speaking to the next. Every story then becomes about whether that conversation is preserved, distorted, interrupted, or rediscovered.

That metaphor is broad enough to support intimate character dramas, archaeological mysteries, political thrillers, and galaxy-spanning epics without ever feeling repetitive. It gives the universe a philosophical identity distinct from most existing science fiction.
