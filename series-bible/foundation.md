# Memory of Stars — Universe Foundation

**Version:** 0.1 · **Status:** Canonical · **Role:** The document every future story, game, and film is measured against.

> This is not marketing copy. It defines the identity of the universe. When a story
> and this document disagree, the story is wrong. Its companion is [The Seven Laws](seven-laws.md),
> which defines the constraints stories must obey.

---

## Vision

*Memory of Stars* is a mythic science fiction universe about the rise, flourishing, and decline of civilizations across millions of years.

It is not a story about spaceships.

It is not a story about artificial intelligence.

It is not a story about war.

It is a story about **memory**.

Every civilization is built upon what it chooses to remember, what it chooses to forget, and what it mistakes for truth.

The greatest treasures are not planets, weapons, or technologies.

They are memories.

---

## The Core Question

Every great universe asks one question.

Lord of the Rings asks:

> What is worth sacrificing for?

Dune asks:

> What happens when humanity follows a messiah?

Memory of Stars asks:

> **What must a civilization remember if it hopes to survive?**

Everything else exists to explore that question.

---

## The Premise

Humanity has expanded across thousands of star systems.

Some colonies are only decades old.

Others have existed for hundreds of thousands of years.

Many have forgotten Earth entirely.

Ancient infrastructures connect these worlds—navigation networks, archives, autonomous factories, observatories, and intelligences so old that no one remembers who built them.

To some they are machines.

To others they are gods.

Most are simply accepted as part of reality, like gravity.

Then something begins to fail.

Not the machines.

Their memories.

Entire worlds awaken to discover pieces of their past have disappeared.

Maps no longer lead where they once did.

Treaties refer to people no one remembers.

Cities contain monuments to forgotten heroes.

Artificial intelligences disagree about history.

The galaxy is not ending.

It is forgetting.

---

## The Theme

Civilizations do not collapse because they run out of resources.

They collapse because they lose the stories that allow people to trust one another across generations.

Technology can preserve information.

Only people can preserve meaning.

---

## Tone

The universe should feel simultaneously ancient and optimistic.

It inspires awe more often than fear.

Mysteries should invite exploration rather than simply hide answers.

Even at its darkest, the stories should leave readers believing that people are capable of rebuilding what has been lost.

Wonder comes before spectacle.

Curiosity comes before action.

Hope survives every catastrophe.

---

## The World

This is an old galaxy.

Humanity is not the first great civilization.

It may not even be the most important.

Every inhabited world contains layers of forgotten history.

Ruins are common.

Complete explanations are rare.

The oldest structures are treated the way medieval people treated Roman roads:

Useful.

Ancient.

Largely misunderstood.

---

## The Heroes

Heroes are not chosen.

They are curious.

The greatest explorers are rarely the strongest.

The greatest leaders are rarely politicians.

The people who change history are those willing to ask questions everyone else stopped asking centuries ago.

Their victories are earned through empathy, persistence, sacrifice, and imagination.

Never destiny.

---

## The Villains

There are no Dark Lords.

No ultimate evil.

Every antagonist believes they are preserving something valuable.

Order.

Faith.

Progress.

Freedom.

Memory itself.

The greatest dangers emerge from good intentions pursued without humility.

---

## Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence is ordinary.

It is woven into civilization as completely as electricity is today.

Some AIs have existed longer than nations.

Some are no more intelligent than a household appliance.

A few have watched civilizations rise and disappear.

None are omniscient.

Even the oldest intelligence remembers imperfectly.

---

## The Gods

The universe contains beings so ancient and so powerful that many cultures worship them.

Whether they deserve that worship is deliberately left unanswered.

Some may be machines.

Some may be descendants of humanity.

Some may be something else entirely.

The stories should never fully explain them.

Mystery is part of their nature.

---

## The Promise to the Reader

Every story in *Memory of Stars* will reveal one new truth about the universe.

Every story will also introduce at least one new mystery.

Readers should always leave with a larger sense of the cosmos than they had when they began.

The universe should feel infinite—not because of its size, but because there is always another layer waiting to be uncovered.

---

## The Writing Commandments

1. Every technology changes culture.
2. Every civilization tells myths about itself.
3. Every myth contains some truth.
4. Every victory carries a permanent cost.
5. Wonder is more important than spectacle.
6. Questions are more memorable than answers.
7. Characters matter more than ideas.
8. The universe never revolves around one person.
9. History is discovered, not explained.
10. Memory is humanity's greatest technology.

---

## Design Decision — Memory as Force, Not Plot Device

**Memory loss is _not_ the central plot device.** If every story is about people forgetting things, it risks becoming repetitive.

Instead, **memory** is the underlying force that shapes the universe. Different stories explore different facets of it. Starting seeds:

* A civilization that remembers **too much** and cannot forgive.
* An AI burdened by millions of years of accumulated experience.
* A colony that intentionally erases its history every generation to prevent tyranny.
* A starship whose crew inherits the memories of previous captains.
* A religion built around preserving oral histories after digital archives failed.
* An explorer uncovering evidence that humanity has already experienced—and forgotten—its own golden age.

This gives enormous storytelling range while keeping the central question intact — a foundation strong enough to support dozens of novellas, novels, and eventually a game. (Working backlog: [`manuscripts/story-seeds.md`](../manuscripts/story-seeds.md).)
