# Craft Notes — Adrian Tchaikovsky

Distilled from his interview on David Perell's *How I Write* (2026). Tchaikovsky has
written 60+ books and novellas and won the Clarke Award for *Children of Time*; his
process is unusually close to this project's — world-first, constraint-driven,
information-logistics planning — which makes his divergences as instructive as his
agreements. Hold everything here the way he holds it: **any maxim is useful only
until it becomes law.**

## 1. World first — and the world writes the story

Build the world before the plot, robustly enough that it extends past the pages.
His origin for this is running role-playing games: *you make the world strong
because you don't know what the players will break* — and a book's readers are
players too. Two working rules fall out:

- **The stone in the pool.** Start from one *what if*, then follow the ripples:
  if that is true, therefore this; and therefore this. Every part of the world
  should trace back to the stone. Ground-up, so that by the time you outline,
  the world already has "solid ground under your feet." If the ground never
  firms up, don't write the book.
- **A world is a moment, not a monument.** You are not describing how things are
  and will be for a thousand years — you are describing the one unstable moment
  just before the book starts. Build the flashpoints, pressures, and fractures
  *into* the setting, and the world will tell you what the story is: the plot is
  where the cracks give. Characters, too, grow out of the weave with their
  strings already attached — the pawnbroker arrives already owing the occupiers
  and the resistance; you don't wire relationships on by hand afterward.

## 2. What makes a what-if worth a book

- **Never repeat someone else's work.** Know the crowded idea spaces (he has
  never written a dragon — too many good dragons exist); enter one only with a
  genuinely new spin, otherwise go elsewhere.
- **Most ideas are half a book.** Keep a file of them. Books happen when two
  halves *click* — uplifted octopuses (half a book) plus an alien that
  interfaces with human neurology (half a book) made *Children of Ruin*. If an
  idea won't grow past a novella's worth, let it be a novella.
- Monsters that tell us about us beat monsters. (Smaug is greed before he is a
  lizard.)

## 3. Planning — a staircase you're allowed to leave

- His plan is a plain document: chapter by chapter, beat by beat. No corkboard
  romance. Down to, where needed: *this conversation must move this fact between
  these characters or the back half can't work.* His strongest line on outlining:
  **"Getting information to the right place is 90% of planning a book."**
- The plan is a staircase, not a cage. Step off it for a better idea; step back
  on. Expect to discover mid-book that some logic — of world or of narrative —
  fails, and pay the cost of rework rather than shipping a book whose business
  turns out not to matter.
- **Fix the penultimate scene; never plan the final one.** Get the heroes into
  the last room, then let the accumulated momentum of the whole book dictate how
  it ends. The ending is the most important part — it's what readers are left
  holding — and it must feel like the only ending the book could have: the
  logical result of everything, and still a surprise. An imposed ending reads
  imposed.

## 4. The left wall and the one big lie

- **The left wall:** what science (or your chosen period, or your magic system)
  says is possible is a wall at your back — you build outward from it, never
  through it. Research is surveying the wall's contours, and the danger is the
  *unknown unknowns*: the things you don't know you don't know, discovered
  three-quarters through a draft.
- **Ask the person who knows.** For water-filled octopus spaceships he found a
  submarine designer; for the spiders, a full day with the Natural History
  Museum's entomologists. Experts love to be asked. The reward is the table
  whose legs all touch the floor.
- **The one big lie:** every speculative book gets one convenient impossibility
  (his: a nanovirus compressing evolution from hundreds of millions of years to
  tens of thousands). Everything *around* the lie must be true, because the
  truth is what makes the lie land. Plausibility isn't pride — it's the source
  of the book's weight: readers close *Children of Time* feeling *this could
  happen*, which magic talking spiders could never buy.
- **Know your position on the continuum before you start** — hardest-SF to
  space opera to full fantasy is one axis of how much wall you keep. Hard SF
  means accepting what your book cannot contain (no time travel; months between
  planets). Fantasy means building the wall yourself: magic with rules, limits,
  and above all **a price** — priceless power dissolves both problem and story
  (his example: Moorcock's bored end-time gods).

## 5. Cities versus travelogues

Travelogue narratives let you walk away from your messes — the next map-point
absolves you of Bree. A city story refuses the absolution: the cast are residents
with lifelong entanglements, and when someone's tolerance finally snaps, they must
stay and live inside the consequences. Costlier to build (you need the
red-strings board *before* drafting: who hates whom, who owes whom), and
commensurately richer: small acts taken for petty reasons dominoing into a city
on fire. Choose consequence-dense ground when you can.

## 6. Fight scenes

- **Your audience is educated.** Fantasy readers include people who know which
  end of the sword goes where; break armor logic (stabbing a broken blade
  through a breastplate) and they throw the book. Learn enough to stab the
  armpit instead — the gap that must exist for the arm to move.
- **Perspective and flow beat technique.** The best battle he cites is seen
  through the slot of one visor: cacophony, collision, opponents swapping
  mid-chaos. Three distinct toolkits: the *battle* (visor-slot chaos plus,
  where earned, the general's overview), the *skirmish* (a dozen combatants
  trading partners — literally block it out with toys if you must), and the
  *duel* (where technique itself becomes the narrative).
- **The fight is a vehicle, or it's filler.** Let it advance character (the
  better fighter declines the killing blow out of fondness — and dies for it:
  that's development, not choreography) and showcase world (shapeshifters
  cycling forms mid-melee).
- **Put as little of your research on the page as possible.** Nobody wants to
  read a fencing manual. The knowing shows through in a handful of words that
  could only have come from someone who knows — those words make it real.
- **Write the feeling before the footwork.** From live-action battles: a
  thousand people screaming downhill at you registers in the body even when
  it's rubber swords. *That* — what a fight feels like from inside — is what
  goes on the page.

## 7. The three-sided knowledge structure

Author, character, reader: three parties who each know different things, and the
gaps between them are an engine.

- Character knows more than reader → the withholding narrator (his Maltese-Falcon
  city where no POV admits to holding the prize — which he wrote agnostically,
  not deciding who had it until the book told him).
- Reader knows more than character → dramatic irony as dread or tragedy: the
  post-tech villagers walking unrecognized ruins; Rex the bioengineered "good
  dog" proudly reporting what the reader recognizes as war crimes. The reader
  stays a step ahead and chews their nails.
- Play the triangle deliberately; decide per book which gaps you're running.

## 8. Maxims, growth, and the subconscious

- **Every writer works differently, and received wisdom handed down as
  commandments is irresponsible.** Show-don't-tell can be wrong (*Children of
  Time* is "60% exposition by volume" and is his most successful book). The
  hero's journey is one structure, not the structure — reduce every story to it
  and the model becomes worthless the way any over-simplified model does. Hold
  all advice — including this file — with a light grip.
- **Improve by naming the next territory.** Per book, one or two definite new
  things: "the book where I do the mosaic structure"; the not-yet-achieved goal
  of the numinous (*Piranesi*, *Mythago Wood*) kept openly on the list. Read
  peers over greats: the living conversation already contains the greats.
- **Write in partnership with your subconscious.** The conscious mind builds the
  framework; the subconscious fills the gaps between the map-points and
  surfaces connections before you're aware of them — characters revealing
  romances that were never planned, "a gap of exactly that shape they move to
  fill." Delegate to it deliberately; experience earns the faith that it will
  deliver.
- **Emotion reaches readers through inhabited characters.** Every character —
  bit part or lead — is real to themselves: wants, fears, reactions. Wear the
  mask, see through its filter, and cast the emotional net wide; you'll never
  hit every reader identically, but a true-to-itself character lands most of
  them in the territory you intend.

## How this maps to the Memory of Stars practice

Where we already agree (keep doing it): world-first with the story told by the
world's fractures (the bible, the atlas, the mesh — our stone-in-the-pool is the
Erasure-as-maintenance); the left wall (cosmology's constraints-not-technology
doctrine); the one big lie (temporal transmitters — everything around them plays
honest); information logistics as the heart of outlining (our canon-ledgers and
"where each reveal lands" schedules); the three-sided knowledge structure (our
reader-only devices — Elias's blank, the mark — are the triangle played at series
scale); research worn lightly (our page guards: "the words 'beam-riding' and
'magsail' stay in the bible"); characters grown from the world with strings
attached (casts derived from each book's institutions).

Where we consciously diverge (and what to borrow anyway): **we do plan endings**
— the outlines fix them, because thirty books must converge — but borrow his
ending discipline at the drafting layer: treat the outlined ending as the
penultimate certainty and let the final beats, the last lines, the exact landing
arise from the book's own momentum (the Shape Rule already grants this: the grid
serves the book). And borrow his warning outright: if a drafted book's ending
makes its middle pointless, the fix is rework, not shipping.

Fight scenes barely exist in the legendarium (two wars, one Scouring) — but §6 is
required reading before drafting Books 9, 17, and 28, and doubly so for the
successor-era works, which will claim the kinetic register on purpose.
