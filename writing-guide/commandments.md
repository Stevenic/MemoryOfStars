# The Writer's Card

Everything a Memory of Stars writer should be able to recite from memory. This is a
**quick reference** — the canonical sources are [`foundation.md`](../series-bible/foundation.md)
and [`seven-laws.md`](../series-bible/seven-laws.md) in the series bible.

## The core question

> **What must a civilization remember if it hopes to survive?**

## The organizing metaphor

> **Civilization is a conversation across generations.**

## The Ten Writing Commandments

1. Every technology changes culture.
2. Every civilization tells myths about itself.
3. Every myth contains some truth.
4. Every victory carries a permanent cost.
5. Wonder is more important than spectacle.
6. Questions are more memorable than answers.
7. Characters matter more than ideas.
8. The universe never revolves around one person.
9. History is discovered, not explained.
10. Memory is humanity's greatest technology.

## The Seven Laws (constraints — [full text](../series-bible/seven-laws.md))

1. **Distance Is Real** — travel is possible, never free.
2. **Memory Outlives Flesh** — everything leaves memories; the question is who can still read them.
3. **Intelligence Is Not Life** — consciousness takes many forms; none is superior.
4. **Technology Changes Culture** — every important invention reshapes society, or it isn't important.
5. **Every Civilization Forgets Something** — memory is always incomplete; truth is recoverable, never obvious.
6. **There Are No Chosen Ones** — history comes from choices, not destiny.
7. **Wonder Comes First** — every story earns one "Wow."

## The Writer's Test (the acceptance gate)

Before a novella is accepted:

- [ ] Teaches one new truth about the universe.
- [ ] Leaves one important mystery unsolved.
- [ ] Permanently changes at least one character.
- [ ] Changes civilization itself, even a little.
- [ ] Makes the universe feel larger at the end than at the start.

If not — rewrite it.

## The Easy-Read Standard (the author's directive)

**Every novella is an easy read.** Not simple — *easy*: a reader who is not a big reader
finishes it in two or three evenings and never once feels lost, hazed, or assigned
homework. This standard outranks every craft device and every series ambition; when
cleverness and readability conflict, cleverness loses.

Checkable rules:

- [ ] **A novella is a 2–3 evening read** (~30k words); **a chapter is one sitting**
      (10–15 minutes) that earns the next.
- [ ] **One POV per novella.** The reader always knows whose eyes they're behind.
- [ ] **Small cast:** roughly 6–8 named characters, every name short and sayable on the
      first try.
- [ ] **Standalone-first:** every book reads cold, from page one, with no prior book
      required. Series echoes reward returning readers; they never gate understanding.
- [ ] **The page never requires the glossary.** New coined terms are few per book and
      taught by scene and context, not definition.
- [ ] **No hazing:** no untranslated jargon walls, no in-medias-res confusion held as a
      virtue, no puzzle-plots that demand note-taking. Mystery is what the *characters*
      haven't solved — never what the *sentence* refuses to say plainly.
- [ ] **Clarity is the voice.** Plain diction and flowing paragraphs (see
      [voice.md](voice.md)) are the accessibility mechanism, not just an aesthetic.

The depth is in the layers underneath — canon, echoes, devices — and the layers are
**optional by design**. A casual reader gets a complete, moving story; a close reader
gets the universe. Both must be true of every book.

## The Chapter-Two Rule (where the hook lives)

**Chapter 1 must be great; chapter 2 must be better** — because chapter 2 is where the
reader decides whether to finish the book. Chapter 1 is read on faith (picking up the
book granted it that much); chapter 2 is read on judgment.

The series shape already encodes this — keep it deliberate: the **prologue intrigues**,
**chapter 1 makes the reader care** (a person, a place, a want), and **chapter 2
detonates the premise** (the impossible hail; the Mercy goes public; the counts that
disagree). Budget Act I accordingly: the best material before the midpoint belongs in
chapter 2.

Checkable:

- [ ] Chapter 2 contains the book's inciting detonation **or** its sharpest scene so far
      — never setup, transition, or parked worldbuilding.
- [ ] Chapter 2 ends on a question the reader would pay to have answered.
- [ ] If chapter 2 is the book's weakest early chapter, the outline is mis-budgeted —
      fix the outline, not the prose.

## The Shape Rule (the length is the law)

The reader's contract is **time, not architecture**: a novella is a 2–3 evening read and
a chapter is one sitting that earns the next. Hold those, and nothing else about the
shape is owed.

- [ ] **Total ~28–34k words**; chapters ~2,500–3,800 (one sitting each). Roughly 8–11
      chapters fall out of that arithmetic — an expectation, never a target.
- [ ] **The grid is chosen per book, in the outline** — and the outline says why, in one
      line. Book 1's founding shape (Prologue + 3 acts × 3 + Epilogue) is always
      available, never owed. Prologue/epilogue only if the story wants them.
- [ ] **Chapter 2 detonates regardless of shape** (the Chapter-Two Rule above).
- [ ] **No book outgrows its slot.** A story that wants more words than the slot holds
      belongs to a later cycle, not a longer book.
