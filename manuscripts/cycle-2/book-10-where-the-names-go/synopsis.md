# Where the Names Go — Synopsis

**Spoiler-complete.** Keep in sync with the [series bible](../../../series-bible/) and the
[canon ledger](canon-ledger.md).

On promotion night, a dozen new lights climb Carath's sky as the re-routed relays come
on, and the city celebrates becoming the reach's newest hub. A few years later, Ivet —
junior registrar of the Hall of Rolls, a woman who knows everyone's name and almost no
one's face — watches the wrongness arrive at her counter: the census re-run disagrees
with itself; a child comes to re-register a grandmother because the stone is blank; and
a woman loses her street's name out of her own mouth, mid-sentence. Real time. Small.
Absolute.

Authority flails; then the guild's responders arrive carrying a protocol nobody knew
existed — duplicate everything, speak everything, plural-copy the rolls — and their
chief, Noor, drafts Ivet as liaison. The rite-book's origin is the cycle's mystery,
resolved: decades of the patient mail, opened on their dates — plain warnings and pages
of practice, converging on Carath — posted from within the network's own architecture by
watchers who cannot steer, only mail. Who and what the watchers are, nobody learns. Most
of their letters were misfiled for years. That, too, is the theme.

The taking has an order: handles before things — signs, then stones, then rolls, then
mouths; the famous name of Carath itself begins to slip. At the midpoint, beneath the
Hall of Rolls, the city's oldest substructure wakes and *fights*: a district's names
simply stop failing for one hour while stone older than the city runs warm — the first
direct sight of both sides of a war older than settlement — then the flicker gutters,
having spent something to buy them the night. The responders deploy the rite-book's last
page, the Night of Strangers: every person speaks their name to two strangers and takes
theirs in return, and Ivet — who knows the names and not the faces — walks the squares
all night, matching them. The child finally tells Ivet her name.

At the crest, what is singly held goes — the famous name of Carath entire — and what is
plurally held holds. Dawn's arithmetic: the names spoken to strangers in time survived,
every one; the saved list is exactly as long as the crowd's courage. Nothing taken
returns. The world that outlived its own name takes new ones — plural, by custom, on
purpose — and Ivet opens the new register with her own two names as its first entry.
The responders' last duty is the final letter in the Carath file, arrived nineteen years
ago, dated yesterday: a thank-you, unsigned, its routing stamp a relay every registry
lists as dark for three centuries — closing with a single line that empties the room:
the city's lost name, written whole. Not restored; *held*, somewhere beneath another
sky. A scholar-order that interviews old minds requisitions a copy of the report.
