## Chapter 2 — Tomas Knows Everything

The Hall of Names smelled of stone dust and candlewax. People spoke quietly here. Not because anyone had asked them to. Because every voice seemed to linger among the carved names, as though the walls remembered conversations long after the speakers had forgotten them.

Lyra stood where Elias had left her. The old caretaker had disappeared into the crowd as quickly as he had unsettled her.

Tomas nudged her shoulder. “You’ve stopped again.”

“I know.”

“He was looking at you. He knew the bird. And then he acted like he didn’t.” Tomas counted the offenses off on his fingers.

“I noticed.” Lyra’s thumb was moving over the chipped wing in her pocket. She made it stop.

Tomas frowned. “I dislike contradictions.”

“You dislike mysteries you can’t solve.”

“I’ve already solved several.”

Lyra smiled. “How many?”

“Three.”

“There aren’t three mysteries.”

“There are at least eleven.”

He produced a folded notebook from his satchel and flipped to a fresh page. “Mystery one, he recognized the bird. Mystery two, he corrected himself almost immediately.” He wrote as he spoke, in tiny handwriting. “Mystery three.” He looked up and waited until she looked back. “Whatever frightened him wasn’t the bird.”

Lyra looked toward the far end of the hall, where Elias stood speaking with a clerk as though nothing in the world had recently refused to make sense. “What was it, then?”

Tomas closed the notebook. “Unestablished.”

“You could say you don’t know.”

“I said unestablished.”

“They mean the same thing.”

“They do not.” He squared the notebook against the flap of his satchel. “One is a hole. The other is a hole I intend to fill.”

“Has anyone ever heard you say it? The other one?”

“There has never been a reason to.”

“One day something will make you say it.”

“When it does, I’ll write it down.”

The registration line was still forming. Tomas glanced along it, then took her sleeve. “You haven’t seen the list.”

The year’s roll hung on a board of pale wood beside the doors, the names written top to bottom in one column, in a clerk’s careful hand. The ink had a faint shine where it had gone on thick. Lyra found her own name a third of the way down, between two names she had known all her life, and touched it before she knew she meant to.

Tomas went down the column with his pencil not quite touching the paper. At the bottom he turned and came back up the other way.

“Forty-three,” he said.

“There are forty-two of us.”

“I am aware. This list had forty-three names at dawn. I counted it twice before breakfast.” He went down it a third time. “It still does.”

“Then a clerk wrote someone twice.”

“No name repeats.”

“Then read me the stranger.”

“I have tried. I read for a stranger twice this morning. Every name I checked belonged to a Pilgrim I know.” He tapped the board’s frame, once. “I checked forty-two names. There were still forty-three.”

Lyra read the column herself, from the top. Every name had a face she could put to it. None was missing. None was strange. She counted as she went, and at the bottom she stopped, and the cold of the black stone reached her through her boots.

Forty-three.

Tomas opened the notebook and wrote the count at the top of a clean page, in figures and in words, and turned it toward her. “Read it back.”

“Forty-three.”

“Again.”

“Forty-three, Tomas.”

“Good.” He shut the notebook. “A number two people have read is harder to lose.”

“You’re not telling the clerks?”

“After the signing. If forty-three sign, the count of Pilgrims is wrong. If forty-two sign, the list is. Either way, I’ll know which record to distrust.”

A clerk called the first names to the tables.

Registration occupied most of the morning. Each Pilgrim approached a long table where three clerks confirmed names, family records, and the small collection of belongings each traveler intended to carry.

“No weapons beyond a walking knife,” one clerk repeated for what sounded like the hundredth time. “No written histories.” “No family heirlooms that cannot be replaced.” “No animals.”

The rules had remained unchanged for generations. No one seemed to know why.

When Lyra reached the table, the eldest clerk smiled kindly. “Lyra Ash.” She confirmed her age, her household, her health, while the clerk marked each answer with a charcoal stylus.

“And what will you carry?”

“Food,” Lyra said. “A blanket. A rain cloak. A cup. A knife. Spare stockings.”

The stylus ticked down the page, one soft stroke for each. The clerk read the list back to her. It took no time at all.

“Is the list complete?”

The bird sat against her ribs, no heavier than a folded letter. Her thumb found the chipped wing through the wool.

“It’s complete,” she said.

The clerk set the stylus down and looked up — at her, not at the record of her. “You understand that the Pilgrimage is voluntary.”

Every Pilgrim received the same question. Everyone already knew the answer. Still, the clerk waited — properly waited, hands folded — as though the question were being asked for the first time in the history of the world.

“I understand.”

“And you choose to make the ascent of your own free will?”

“I do.”

The clerk slid a narrow silver thread across the table. “Then welcome.”

Both answers went into the record in the same gray ink.

Walking away, she passed the second table, where Tomas was mid-interview. His eyes came off his clerk and found her, dropped once to her coat pocket, and came back up. She held them. Then he went on listing the contents of his pack, in order of weight, without a word out of place.

She wondered, for the first time in her life, what would happen if a Pilgrim said no. She had never heard of one who had. She could not decide which was stranger: that the question was always asked, or that it had never once been needed.

She tied the thread around her wrist. It weighed nothing. The bird weighed a little more than nothing. She was aware of both for the rest of the day.

Outside, Lyra waited beneath a maple tree at the edge of the square. Tomas emerged resettling his satchel, sat down beside her, opened his notebook to the day’s page, and put it away again with nothing added.

“How long did they keep you?” she asked.

“Eight minutes.”

“You probably answered questions they didn’t ask.”

“I provided useful context.”

“You always provide useful context.”

“They should appreciate thoroughness.”

“They do not,” she said, watching his face.

He sighed. “They rarely do.”

Before they could leave, a familiar voice called from behind them. “Lyra.”

Elias stood in the doorway. Up close, he looked even older than he had across the hall. Lines crossed his face like rivers on an old map. His silver staff rested lightly in one hand, polished smooth from decades of use.

“I owe you an apology,” he said.

“For what?”

“I startled you.”

“A little.”

He nodded. “I’ve been… remembering poorly today.”

Lyra waited. It seemed the sort of sentence that deserved an explanation.

None came.

Instead Elias glanced at her coat pocket, where the red bird was tucked. Then his eyes rose to hers, and waited. “May I?”

She handed it to him. He turned it carefully in his hands. His thumb paused over the chipped wing.

“It has been well loved.”

“My father made it.”

“So you said.”

He turned the bird over once more, slower than the first time, and his shoulders came down half an inch. “He did good work.” He returned the bird. “Take good care of it.”

“I always have.”

“I know.”

Again Elias went still, like a man stopped halfway through a sentence.

Tomas had given up pretending not to watch. “Elias?”

The old man turned. “Yes?”

“Did you know my mother?”

“Sel. Of course.”

“You remembered her quickly.”

Elias chuckled. “I remember many things quickly.”

“And slowly?”

The smile faded just enough to notice. “Those are the troublesome ones.”

Tomas reached into his satchel. “I have something.”

He withdrew an old brass compass. Its glass was cracked. The needle wandered lazily instead of settling north.

“It was my mother’s.”

Elias stared at it. For several seconds he said nothing. Then he took the compass with both hands, the way you take something that is still warm.

“She carried this.”

“Every journey,” Tomas said.

“So she did.”

Elias tapped the cracked glass once, gently. The needle trembled. For an instant it pointed—not north—but toward the mountain. Then it spun aimlessly again.

“Strange,” Elias murmured.

“It hasn’t worked since she died,” Tomas said.

“Perhaps it still does.” He handed it back. “But not in the way it was made to.”

Tomas opened his mouth. Closed it again. Even he seemed unsure how to respond.

At midday the last Pilgrim signed, and the clerks called them all back to the steps for the reading of the roll. The reading closed every registration day in living memory: the year’s names read aloud where they hung, each answered by its owner, so the city would know its Pilgrims. Lyra had watched it from other crowds in other years.

Families gathered below the steps. The eldest clerk stood at the board and set her finger under the first name.

“Anet.”

“Here.”

“Cor.”

“Here.”

Name after name went up, and answer after answer came back, and the exchange found a rhythm, and the rhythm was the pleasant kind — the kind that belonged to ovens and looms and morning bells. When her own name came, Lyra’s hand was in her pocket. She took it out to answer.

Near the end, Tomas answered his without raising his eyes from the notebook, where his pencil was ticking off the names as they were read.

The clerk came to the bottom of the column and lifted her finger from the paper. “Forty-two. The roll is complete.”

“It is not.”

The square did not go quiet all at once. Quiet moved through it the way cold moves through an open door.

Tomas climbed the steps with the notebook in one hand and the pencil in the other. “At dawn this roll held forty-three names. I counted it twice before the doors opened. I counted it twice again at midmorning, and my count was confirmed by a second counter.”

The eldest clerk’s eyes went past him and found Lyra.

“Forty-three,” Lyra said. “I counted it myself.”

The clerk did not argue. She turned to the board and counted aloud, her finger under each name, and the count was taken up below her — parents first, then Pilgrims, then the children on shoulders who thought it a game, the whole square counting in one slow voice the way it sang at festivals — and the voice climbed through thirty, through forty, through forty-one, and arrived at forty-two, and stopped, because there was nowhere else for it to go.

“Count it yourself,” the clerk said, and stepped aside.

Tomas counted from the top. Forty-two. From the bottom. Forty-two. He came at the column out of its middle, both directions at once, the way he checked his own change. The number sat at forty-two and did not move.

“Then a name has come off it,” someone said. “Whose?”

They began raising names — sons and daughters, the smith’s boy, the twins from the mill row — and every name anyone raised was on the roll, and had a Pilgrim under it, and had already answered for itself once that day. Every name anyone could think of was there. Whatever was gone was not among the things that could be thought of.

A woman near the front said the reading had felt long to her — longer than forty-two names. She could not say why. She had not counted. It had used more of the morning than it should have.

Tomas opened his notebook, to set his morning count against the board’s. He got the page half turned toward the clerk. Then he brought it back and held it close to his face.

The count at the top of the page read forty-two. In figures and in words. In his own tiny hand.

“This is my handwriting,” he said.

Nobody argued with him.

He turned the page toward Lyra without being asked. She had watched him write that number. She had read it back to him twice, because a number two people have read is harder to lose. The memory was still whole — the pencil, the figures, the words — and under the memory the page said what it said. There was nothing in her hands to hold against it.

“Elias,” the eldest clerk said.

He had been standing beneath the arch of the doors. He came up the steps one at a time. The crowd made the kind of room for him it made for no one else in Orin — he knew everyone; he had always known everyone — and he stood before the board and read the roll from the top, his lips moving with the names the way they must have moved over births and deaths for fifty years.

At the bottom he stopped. He did not turn around.

“Whose was it, Elias?” the clerk asked.

His hand moved at his side, searching for the staff it was already holding.

“I don’t know.”

All her life Lyra had heard him say those words, and they had always sounded like an apology.

They did not sound like one now.

The clerks did the sensible things. They counted the morning’s signatures: forty-two. They counted the threads cut from the silver spool: forty-two. They took the roll down and held the paper to the light. The names filled the page evenly, top to bottom, no crowding and no gap — if a name had gone out of it, the paper had closed over the place where it had been.

They hung the roll back on its board. The crowd did not disperse so much as change the subject. There was dough rising in a hundred kitchens, there were stalls standing half open, there were children announcing themselves hungry, and by ones and twos the square let itself be ordinary again.

On the steps, Tomas printed the new count beneath the old one and studied the two numbers, in the same hand, on the same page. He crossed out neither.

When Lyra turned at the mouth of the street, Elias was still at the board, reading the roll from the top again.

She took the bird out of her pocket and walked with it in her fist.

They went through the market without talking. Twice, Tomas’s hand went to the satchel flap and came away without opening it.

The stalls were crowded now. Pilgrims moved through the streets wearing fresh silver threads around their wrists, easy to recognize even without them. Shopkeepers called them by name. Neighbors offered small gifts. A baker pressed warm rolls into Tomas’s hands despite his protests. A florist tucked a white blossom behind Lyra’s ear.

An elderly man she’d never met clasped her shoulders and said, “Carry us well.” Before she could ask what he meant, he had already disappeared into the crowd.

“They’re all doing it,” Lyra said.

“What?”

“Talking as though they know something.”

“They do know something.” He said it around a mouthful of the baker’s roll he had sworn he didn’t want.

“What?”

Tomas frowned. “Unestablished.”

“That’s twice today.”

“I’m aware of the count.”

That afternoon they climbed the western wall overlooking Orin. It had become their place years ago. From there they could see nearly the entire city.

The river. The market. The towers. Beyond everything else—

The mountain.

Clouds wrapped around its summit. Not thick enough to hide it. Only enough to suggest it preferred a little privacy.

Tomas unfolded a sheet of paper.

“You brought diagrams.”

“I always bring diagrams.”

He spread the page across the stones and pinned its corners with pebbles: the roll, written out four times in his tiny hand, column beside column.

“From memory,” he said. “Without looking, and an hour apart. I thought my hand might keep what my head lost.”

“Did it?”

“Forty-two. All four times.” He set his finger in the margin between two columns. “The inventory is short. I can prove the shortage. I cannot produce the item.”

Lyra spread her hand flat on the stone. “Say the count was right at dawn. Then somewhere in Orin there’s an empty bed. A coat with nobody in it. A bowl too many at someone’s table.”

“Or nothing is missing, and the record was wrong.”

“Do you believe that?”

“It is the sensible explanation.”

“Do you believe it?”

He moved one pebble until it sat square with the corner of the page. “Unestablished.”

Below them the city went about its afternoon, and the sound of it came up thin, like a conversation heard through a wall.

“I should have copied it,” Tomas said. “I wrote the sum and not the parts. I won’t make that mistake twice.”

“You know,” she said, “one day you’ll discover the Pilgrimage is exactly what everyone says it is.”

“I consider that statistically unlikely.”

“What if it’s simply a journey?”

“It isn’t.”

“How do you know?”

“Because nobody returns willing to describe the ending.”

Lyra turned back toward the mountain. She had never thought about it that way.

She thought of every adult she had ever asked. Master Rian had described his boots. Mara had described being frightened. The baker had once spent a quarter hour on the subject of which breads traveled well. Not one of them had ever described arriving.

Whenever a conversation approached the summit, it drifted elsewhere. Not abruptly. Almost unconsciously. Like rivers flowing around a stone.

“What if they just can’t explain it?” she asked.

Tomas looked from the page to the mountain. “That,” he admitted, “is possible.”

It was the closest thing to surrender she had ever heard from him.

As shadows lengthened across the city, families began lighting lanterns. Smoke curled upward from hundreds of chimneys. The streets glowed gold beneath the setting sun.

“It’s beautiful,” Lyra said.

“It always has been.”

“I’ve never noticed.”

“You’ve never had reason to think you were leaving.”

“I’m coming back.”

“I know.”

He said it immediately. Without hesitation. Without the strange pause every adult seemed to have.

She smiled. “Thank you.”

“What for?”

“For saying it like you believe it.”

Tomas turned from the city to her. “I do believe it.”

“I know.”

They sat together in comfortable silence.

Then, from the eastern tower, the evening bells began.

Tomas took the compass out and set it on the warm stone between them, turning it until the dial’s north faced north. Through the first bell the needle wandered, lazy as ever. Through the second. Through the third. On the last it drifted — unhurried, as though it had all the time in the world — until it pointed at the mountain.

It stayed there, trembling faintly, for as long as the final note hung in the air.

Then the note faded, and the needle let go.

Neither of them said anything. Tomas put the compass away with both hands.

Far below, Elias crossed the square alone. He stopped. Looked toward the mountain. Then he glanced beside himself, the way you glance at someone to share a thought too small for words.

No one was there.

He stood for another moment. Then continued toward the Hall of Names.

She watched until he disappeared inside.

In seven days, forty-two Pilgrims would climb the mountain.

All the way home, Lyra counted forty-three.
