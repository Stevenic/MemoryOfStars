## Chapter 2 — Tomas Knows Everything

The Hall of Names smelled of stone dust and candlewax. People spoke quietly here. Not because anyone had asked them to. Because every voice seemed to linger among the carved names, as though the walls remembered conversations long after the speakers had forgotten them.

Lyra stood where Elias had left her. The old caretaker had disappeared into the crowd as quickly as he had unsettled her.

Tomas nudged her shoulder. “You’ve stopped again.”

“I know.”

“He recognized the bird. Then he denied it. Then he apologized for the denial.” Tomas counted the offenses off on his fingers. “Three mysteries in one morning. This is an exceptional rate.”

“I was there.”

He had the notebook out of his satchel and was writing as he spoke, in handwriting small enough to keep secrets. “Mystery one, he knew the bird. Mystery two, he corrected himself almost immediately. Mystery three.” He waited until she turned from the crowd and met his eyes. “Whatever frightened him wasn’t the bird.”

“What was it, then?”

“Unestablished.”

“Most people would say they don’t know.”

“Most people stop there.” He closed the notebook. “Unestablished means I intend to establish it.”

The year’s roll hung near the doors, on a board of pale wood — the list he had hurried her through breakfast to see. Names in a clerk’s narrow ink, one beneath another in a single column, and at the top, alone, the leader’s.

“Seren,” Tomas read, in the tone of a man collecting a debt. “No longer adjacent to the truth. Merely the truth.”

Lyra found her own name third from the top. She had never been third at anything. Somewhere in the Hall someone had changed the order and told no one why, and on an ordinary day that would have occupied Tomas until supper.

But he was not reading the names. He was counting them. She could tell by the way his chin dipped at each line, a bow to every entry. At the bottom of the column he stopped. His chin came back up, and he started over. Then he did it a third time.

“Forty-three.”

“There are forty-two Pilgrims.”

“I am aware of what there are.” His eyes stayed on the column. “I am telling you what the list has.”

“Count again.”

“I have counted three times. The number is not embarrassed by repetition.”

Lyra stepped past him and counted for herself — finger under each name, ink and paper-grain under her fingertip, one, two, three, down the whole column without hurrying.

Forty-three.

She stood with her finger below the last name. The paper told her hand what it had told his eyes.

“The Hall of Names has miscounted.” Tomas was trying to keep the delight out of his voice and losing. “Someone has been entered twice, or added who should not be, and I am going to find them.” He wrote it down — she watched the word go into the notebook, forty-three, in his morning hand — and then he drew a folded packet from the satchel. “My census.”

“You keep a census.”

“Forty-two Pilgrims. Names, households, ages to the month.” He set the packet against the board and put his pencil under the first name of the roll. “We will find the stranger before the clerks finish yawning.”

A hand-bell rang from the registration table. The crowd folded itself into lines, and a young clerk read from the new order: “Lyra Ash.”

“After,” Tomas said — to the board more than to her — and the census went back into the satchel.

Registration occupied most of the morning. Each Pilgrim approached the long table where three clerks confirmed names, family records, and the small collection of belongings each traveler intended to carry.

“No weapons beyond a walking knife,” the middle clerk repeated, for what sounded like the hundredth time. “No written histories. No family heirlooms that cannot be replaced. No animals.”

The rules had remained unchanged for generations. No one seemed to know why.

When Lyra reached the table, the eldest clerk smiled. “Lyra Ash.” She confirmed her age, her household, her health, while the clerk marked each answer with a charcoal stylus, and none of it felt like a morning that would matter.

“And what will you carry?”

“Food. A blanket. A rain cloak. A cup. A knife. Spare stockings.”

The stylus kept pace, a dry scratch for each item. From the end of the table came a fainter scratching — Tomas, finished with his own registration after eight thorough minutes, had installed himself where he could observe procedure. The clerks permitted him the way you permit weather.

The eldest clerk reached the bottom of her column. “Anything else?”

The bird lay against her ribs with its small particular weight. Her hand was in her pocket; her thumb had found the chipped wing. She took the hand out and laid it flat on the edge of the table.

“Nothing that can’t be replaced.”

The rule’s own words, handed back. All her life she had turned other people’s sentences over, feeling for the seam. Now, needing one, she had borrowed theirs.

The stylus scratched. The fainter scratching at the end of the table had stopped.

She did not turn her head.

Then the clerk set the stylus down and looked up — at her, not at the record of her. “You understand that the Pilgrimage is voluntary.”

Every Pilgrim received the same question. Everyone already knew the answer. Still, the clerk waited — properly waited, hands folded — as though the question were being asked for the first time in the history of the world.

“I understand.”

“And you choose to make the ascent of your own free will?”

“I do.”

It was true. It sounded no different.

The clerk slid a narrow silver thread across the table. “Then welcome.”

Walking away, Lyra wondered, for the first time in her life, what would happen if a Pilgrim said no. She had never heard of one who had. She could not decide which was stranger: that the question was always asked, or that it had never once been needed.

She tied the thread around her wrist. It weighed nothing. She was aware of it for the rest of the day.

The bird weighed a little more than nothing. She was aware of that too.

Outside, Tomas waited beneath the maple tree. He was not writing. He was not reading. He watched a cart cross the square with an attention it had not earned.

“You’re quiet,” she said.

“I’m counting.”

“Counting what?”

“I’ll know when I finish.”

Before she could ask what that meant, a familiar voice came from the doorway. “Lyra.”

Elias stood there, older up close than he had seemed across the hall. Lines crossed his face like rivers on an old map. The silver staff rested in one hand, polished smooth from decades of use.

“I owe you an apology,” he said.

“For what?”

“I startled you.”

“A little.”

“I’ve been… remembering poorly today.”

Lyra waited. It seemed the sort of sentence that deserved an explanation. None came. Instead his glance dropped to her coat pocket, then rose and held her eyes. “May I?”

She handed the bird over. He turned it in both hands, and his thumb paused at the chipped wing.

“It has been well loved.”

“My father made it.”

“So you said.”

“You’ve seen it before.” She leaned in. “Where?”

“I have seen many birds.” He turned it over once more, slower, and his shoulders came down half an inch. “He did good work.” He returned it. “Take good care of it.”

“I always have.”

“I know.”

Then Elias went still, like a man stopped halfway through a sentence.

Tomas had given up pretending not to watch. “Elias. Did you know my mother?”

“Sel. Of course.”

“You remembered her quickly.”

“I remember many things quickly.”

“And slowly?”

The smile faded just enough to notice. “Those are the troublesome ones.”

Tomas reached into his satchel and brought out an old brass compass. Its glass was cracked. The needle wandered instead of settling north. “It was hers.”

Elias took the compass with both hands, the way you take something that is still warm. “She carried this.”

“Every journey.”

“So she did.” He tapped the cracked glass once. The needle trembled, and for a moment it pointed — not north — toward the mountain. Then it wandered again.

“Strange,” Elias murmured.

“It hasn’t worked since she died,” Tomas said.

“Perhaps it still does.” He handed it back. “But not in the way it was made to.”

Tomas opened his mouth. Closed it. Even he seemed unsure what to file that under.

From inside the hall a hand-bell rang three times: the reading of the roll, the close of every registration, the year’s names read aloud once and answered, the Pilgrims made official. Elias straightened. “They will want the caretaker,” he said, in the tone of a man apologizing for being wanted.

The hall had filled again. Forty-two Pilgrims stood in loose rows before the board, families along the walls, the clerks at their table, Elias beneath the central arch. The eldest clerk read the roll name by name, and each name got its answer — some steady, some barely out of the throat, one boy answering twice in case the first had not counted.

Lyra listened for a name without a face.

Every name had one. Every face was in the hall.

“Forty-two names,” the clerk finished, lowering the paper. “The roll is—”

“Forty-three.”

Tomas did not say it loudly. He said it the way he said all numbers, as though it had been true before he spoke and would stay true after. The hall turned.

The eldest clerk found him in the second row. “The roll holds forty-two names.”

“This morning it held forty-three. I counted three times.” The murmur was already moving along the walls; he raised his voice one measured notch. “Lyra counted after me.”

The clerk’s eyes moved to her.

“With my hand,” Lyra said. “Finger under every name.”

Somewhere behind her a man laughed, and stopped in the middle of it.

The eldest clerk did not laugh. She walked back to the board and set her stylus beneath the first name. “Then we will count,” she said. “Together.”

And the hall counted with her — clerks and Pilgrims and parents, children lifted onto shoulders to see, Elias beneath the arch with both hands on his staff, one slow voice climbing the numbers the way Pilgrims climb the mountain, evenly, all together, no one hurrying, up through the twenties, through the thirties, past forty, and stopping.

“Forty-two.”

Lyra had counted with them. Her own mouth had said it with everyone else’s.

Her finger remembered otherwise.

Tomas walked to the board. The clerk stepped aside for him. He put his finger under the first name, as Lyra had that morning, and went down the column with his lips shaping every number. At the last name his finger stayed where it was.

“Forty-two,” he said, to the paper.

He took out the notebook. He did not hurry. She watched him find the morning’s page and read it over his shoulder, and the entry said, in his handwriting, in ink hours dry: Posted roll, morning of registration. Forty-two names.

No scrape. No blot. No crowding in the letters. The word sat on the line as though it had been born there.

“I wrote forty-three,” he said.

“I watched you write it,” Lyra said.

He turned the page. He turned it back. The page did not change.

The clerks brought out the day’s register and laid it open beside the roll. Forty-two entries in the register. Forty-two names on the roll. Paper agreed with paper, column with column, down the whole morning’s record.

Only the people disagreed, and only two of them.

No one told them they were mistaken. Somehow that was worse.

It was Lyra who turned toward the arch. “Elias.” Her voice carried in that hall whether she asked it to or not. “You keep the names. Whose was the forty-third?”

Elias came forward slowly. He stood before the roll for a long moment. Then he put his finger under the first name, and the caretaker of the names counted them the way a child counts letters — touching each one, lips moving, all the way down the column — while the hall held its breath around him. At the last name his finger stopped and did not lift.

“I don’t know.”

Elias went still.

Not the stillness of remembering.

The stillness of a man listening for a step behind him.

He turned to the eldest clerk. “Your mother’s name.” She blinked, and gave it. “And her mother’s?” She gave that too. He nodded once, like a man testing a rope before trusting his weight to it. The rope held. It did not seem to comfort him.

“Forgive me,” he said.

He said it to the wall of names.

No one argued. That was the strange part. The hall simply began to empty, families gathering their children, no one raising a voice, everyone leaving a little closer together than they had come. By the doors a woman counted her children as they reached her. Three. Lyra saw her count them again.

Tomas stayed at the board and copied the roll entire — all forty-two names into the notebook, in handwriting larger than any she had ever seen him use, pressing hard enough to leave the words standing in the next three pages.

They walked home the long way, because neither of them proposed the short one. Word had outrun them into the market: knots of neighbors loosened as Pilgrims passed and retied behind them. A baker pressed warm rolls on them unasked. An elderly man Lyra had never met clasped her shoulders and said, “Carry us well,” and was gone into the crowd before she could ask what he meant.

That afternoon they climbed the western wall overlooking Orin. It had become their place years ago. From there they could see nearly the whole city — the river, the market, the towers. Beyond everything else—

The mountain.

Clouds wrapped its summit. Not thick enough to hide it. Only enough to suggest it preferred a little privacy.

Tomas sat with the satchel between his boots, flap buckled. No diagrams. No census. In all their years on the wall she had never once sat beside him without paper between them.

“You’re not going to chart it,” she said.

“Every count I have ever made is now provisional. I am deciding what follows from that.”

She held out her hand. “Give me the compass.”

He gave it to her. She turned it over, tilted the cracked glass to the light, weighed it in her palm. The needle wandered, indifferent. The compass sat in her hand with its honest weight, and whatever had happened to the roll had not happened to it.

“One day,” she said, handing it back, “you’ll discover the Pilgrimage is exactly what everyone says it is.”

“Nobody returns willing to describe the ending.”

She thought of every adult she had ever asked. Master Rian had described his boots. Mara had described being frightened. The baker had once spent a quarter hour on which breads traveled well. Not one of them had ever described arriving. Whenever a conversation approached the summit, it drifted elsewhere, the way rivers flow around a stone.

“What if they can’t explain it?”

“That,” he said, “is now one of my better theories.”

Below them the lamplighters were starting their rounds. Smoke stood up from a hundred chimneys, and the streets went gold under the lowering sun.

“What will you carry?” Tomas asked.

“You heard the list.”

“I heard it.” He watched the mountain, not her. “Six things.”

The number sat between them. It was not a large number. Something was missing from it, and each of them knew that the other could count.

“Six things,” she said again.

His eyes went to her coat pocket, held there the length of a breath, and came back to the mountain. He did not open the notebook. It was the first thing she had ever known him to leave uncounted.

“I’m coming back,” she said.

“I know.”

He said it at once, without the pause every adult seemed to have.

“Thank you.”

“What for?”

“For saying it like you believe it.”

He turned from the city to her. “I do believe it.”

Then, from the eastern tower, the evening bells began.

Tomas set the compass on the warm stone between them, turning it until the dial’s north faced north. Through the first bell the needle wandered, lazy as ever. Through the second. Through the third. On the last it drifted — unhurried, as though it had all the time in the world — until it pointed at the mountain.

It stayed there, trembling faintly, for as long as the final note hung in the air.

Then the note faded, and the needle let go.

Neither of them said anything. Tomas put the compass away with both hands.

Far below, Elias crossed the square alone. He stopped, and glanced beside himself, the way you glance at someone to share a thought too small for words. No one was there. He stood a moment longer, then went on toward the Hall of Names.

Forty-two Pilgrims would climb the mountain in seven days. The roll said so. The hall had said so, with one voice. The notebook in Tomas’s satchel said so now, in his own hand.

All the way home, Lyra counted forty-three.
