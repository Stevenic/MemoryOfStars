## Chapter 2 — Tomas Knows Everything

The Hall of Names smelled of stone dust and candlewax. People spoke quietly here — not because anyone asked them to, but because every voice seemed to linger among the carved names, as though the walls remembered conversations long after the speakers had forgotten them.

Tomas was counting.

He stood at the board beside the doors, where the year’s roll had been posted: the Pilgrims of their year, written on paper in a clerk’s fair hand, one name under another like rungs. His finger moved down the column. At the bottom it returned to the top. Tomas never counted anything twice unless the world had made a mistake.

Lyra left the spot where Elias had abandoned her and crossed to him, past a clerk trailing silver cord, past two mothers holding one conversation in two whispers.

“Count these,” Tomas said. He had not turned around.

“Good morning to you as well.”

“Count them.”

She set her finger where his had been. The paper was cool from the stone, the ink raised where the hand had pressed. She counted under her breath, name by name, to the bottom of the column.

“Forty-three.”

“Again.”

“You’ve counted them yourself.”

“Verification requires a second instrument.”

She counted again, slower. Forty-three names — and she knew every one of them. That was the part she noticed second. Names she had answered to teachers beside, raced against, sat next to at winter suppers. She read the column a third time hunting the stranger, and the column did the thing the arithmetic said it couldn’t.

Every name belonged.

“There are forty-two Pilgrims this year,” Tomas said. “Everyone says so. The clerks say so. I have documented our year since we were six, and we are forty-two.” He opened his notebook and held it flat. There, in handwriting small enough to hide from the world, sat the morning’s entry — the roll, counted at dawn before the doors opened — a number boxed in with four straight strokes, the way he boxed anything he intended to keep.

Forty-three.

“You counted this at dawn,” Lyra said, “and mentioned nothing at breakfast.”

“At breakfast it was a clerical error. Clerical errors are not conversation.”

“And now?”

“Now I have counted five times, the clerks have counted once, and one of us is wrong by a person.”

Her thumb had found the bird through the wool of her pocket without her asking it to. She made it stop. “Ask Elias. He knows everyone.”

“I intend to. He is mystery four.” Tomas counted the others off on his fingers. “He recognized the bird. He corrected himself almost immediately. Whatever frightened him wasn’t the bird.” The fourth finger he pointed at the roll. “And the Hall of Names cannot add.”

“What did frighten him, then?”

“Unestablished.”

“Most people would say ‘I don’t know.’”

“Most people give up early.”

“You realize it’s the rarest sentence in the Tomas language.”

“It is not rare.” He closed the notebook. “It is extinct.”

At the front of the hall, the eldest of the clerks lifted a small hand-bell and rang it once. Lines began to form. Registration would take the morning; the reading of the roll would close it at midday, every name read aloud and answered, so the city could hear who was climbing.

Tomas wrote the count a second time beneath the first and pressed the book shut.

Forty-three, both entries said.

Registration was three clerks at a long table, confirming names, family records, and the small collection of belongings each traveler intended to carry.

“No weapons beyond a walking knife,” the youngest clerk recited, for what sounded like the hundredth time. “No written histories. No family heirlooms that cannot be replaced. No animals.”

The rules had remained unchanged for generations. No one seemed to know why. Lyra heard the list once for every Pilgrim ahead of her; by her turn she could have said it in her sleep, which was perhaps the point.

“Lyra Ash.” The eldest clerk smiled. She confirmed Lyra’s age, her household, her health, and after each answer the charcoal stylus made its small dry sound against the page.

“And what will you carry?”

She named it: food, a blanket, a rain cloak, a cup, a knife, spare stockings. Six ticks of the stylus. Six replaceable things.

“Anything else?”

The bird had been in her coat pocket since morning. She had not put it there — or she had, and the putting had left no mark on her, the way sleep leaves none. She could feel it against her ribs when she breathed. A wooden weight lighter than an egg, and heavier than everything she had named together.

Tomas stood next in line, close enough to count. He counted everything; it was how he listened. Six declared. His eyes were on the seventh — she felt the glance land on her pocket the way you feel sun through wool.

Her thumb found the chipped wing.

“Nothing,” Lyra said.

The stylus came down and made the same small mark it had made all morning.

Then the clerk set it aside and looked up — at her, not at the record of her. “You understand that the Pilgrimage is voluntary.”

Every Pilgrim received the same question. Everyone already knew the answer. Still, the clerk waited — properly waited, hands folded — as though the question were being asked for the first time in the history of the world.

“I understand.”

“And you choose to make the ascent of your own free will?”

“I do.”

It was true. She said it the way you set a true thing down next to a false one: carefully, without letting them touch.

The clerk slid a narrow silver thread across the table. “Then welcome.”

Lyra took the thread and turned. Tomas’s gaze came up from her pocket to her face and held there, one beat, two, while the line waited behind him. Six declared; seven carried; Tomas kept accounts the way other people kept promises. She watched him not do it — not open the notebook, not raise the count, not say the seventh thing into the record. Then he stepped past her to the table and gave the clerks his name.

It should have felt like kindness. It weighed more than the bird.

On the Hall steps she tied the thread around her wrist. It weighed nothing. She was aware of it for the rest of the day.

And she wondered, for the first time in her life, what would happen if a Pilgrim said no. She had never heard of one who had. She could not decide which was stranger: that the question was always asked, or that it had never once been needed.

⸻

Under the maple tree that shaded the steps, she waited for Tomas. He came out at last with his satchel straps squared and his mouth set.

“How long did they question you?”

“Eight minutes.”

“They questioned me for one.”

“You presumably failed to provide context.”

“You always provide context.”

“They should appreciate thoroughness.”

“They do not,” she said, watching his face.

He sighed. “They rarely do.”

Eight minutes of context, and not one word about her pocket. She did not thank him. There was no way to thank him that was not a confession.

“Lyra.”

Elias stood in the doorway. Up close he seemed older than he had across the hall — lines crossed his face like rivers on an old map. The silver staff rested in one hand, polished smooth by decades.

“I owe you an apology,” he said.

“For what?”

“I startled you.”

“A little.”

He nodded. “I’ve been… remembering poorly today.”

Lyra waited. It seemed the sort of sentence that deserved an explanation. None came.

Instead his glance dropped to her coat pocket, then rose and held her eyes. “May I?”

She gave him the bird. He turned it over with care, and his thumb paused at the chipped wing.

“It has been well loved.”

“My father made it.”

“So you said.”

He turned it once more, slower, and his shoulders came down half an inch. “He did good work.” He handed it back. “Take good care of it.”

“I always have.”

“I know.”

Then he went still, like a man stopped halfway through a sentence.

Tomas had given up pretending not to watch. “Elias. Did you know my mother?”

“Sel. Of course.”

“You remembered her quickly.”

“I remember many things quickly.”

“And slowly?”

The smile faded enough to notice. “Those are the troublesome ones.”

Tomas brought out an old brass compass, its glass cracked, its needle wandering instead of settling. “It was my mother’s.”

Elias took it with both hands, the way you take something that is still warm. “She carried this.”

“Every journey.”

“So she did.” He tapped the glass once. The needle trembled — and for a moment it pointed, not north, but at the mountain. Then it wandered again.

“Strange,” Elias murmured.

“It hasn’t worked since she died.”

“Perhaps it still does.” He handed it back. “But not in the way it was made to.”

Tomas opened his mouth, closed it, and filed nothing.

From the doorway, the hand-bell rang twice. The reading of the roll. Elias excused himself with a small bow, and they followed him in.

The hall had filled again. Forty-two Pilgrims stood in loose rows with their families behind them, and the eldest clerk held the roll up to the light, unpinned from its board.

She read the way clerks read, a name and a breath and a name. Each name was answered. Here. Here. A boy’s voice cracked on his, and his friends were shushed for laughing. The reading had a rhythm, like slow bells, and Lyra counted without deciding to.

“Forty-two names,” the clerk said at last, folding the roll. “The roll is read. The—”

“Forty-three,” Tomas said.

The word carried the way dropped metal carries.

The clerk’s face arranged itself the way faces do for boys who interrupt rites. “The roll holds forty-two names.”

“At dawn it held forty-three.” Tomas came forward, notebook open. “I counted before the doors opened. I counted again at mid-morning. She counted twice more.” He tipped his head toward Lyra. “Forty-three names. No repetitions. No strangers. You have read forty-two.”

“Then you miscounted, young man.”

“I have never miscounted.” He said it the way other people said their own names.

The clerk unfolded the roll and went down it with her finger, lips moving, while the hall held its breath around her.

“Forty-two.”

“Aloud,” said a gray-haired woman near the front. Something in her voice made the clerk obey.

She began at the top, aloud this time, and the hall counted with her — parents and Pilgrims and clerks and children hoisted onto shoulders, the whole morning’s crowd keeping the tally in one low voice that climbed a number at a time, past twenty, past thirty, past forty, steady as stairs — and stopped.

“Forty-two,” said the hall.

The gray-haired woman spoke into the quiet. “I counted forty-three this morning. When I brought my son. I decided I’d counted badly, and I didn’t count again.” Her hands smoothed her skirt, over and over, though it lay flat. “I didn’t want to.”

A man by the doors said the extra name had been low on the page — an old family name, he thought. He got as far as the first letter. His mouth stayed open around a sound that never arrived, and he shut it, and studied his shoes as though the name had fallen there.

Tomas turned to his morning page to read the proof aloud, and stopped.

The number sat in its box, four straight strokes around it, in his own small hand.

Forty-two.

He turned back a page, forward a page. He held the notebook close, then at arm’s length, like a coin he suspected. Beneath the first entry the second said the same thing. Forty-two, twice, in handwriting no one else could have imitated, because no one else could write that small.

“I wrote forty-three,” he said. Quietly. To the notebook, not to the hall. “I wrote it twice.”

Lyra went forward and counted the roll herself, finger touching each name. The column was seamless. No gap, no crowding, no half-made letter. If the paper had ever carried another line, the paper had no opinion about it now.

“Elias.” The eldest clerk’s voice had lost its rite. “Whose was the forty-third name?”

He had come forward without Lyra hearing him move. He read the roll from the top, lips shaping forty-two names like a prayer he suspected of missing a verse. Then he read it again.

The hall waited. He was the caretaker of the names. He had known everyone in Orin for longer than their grandparents had been alive, and no one had ever tested it, the way no one tests a bridge they cross daily.

“I don’t know,” Elias said.

A child began a question somewhere and was hushed.

Elias did not go still. That was the worst of it. Stillness was the thing Lyra knew him by, the way she knew Mara by her busy hands — and now his free hand traveled from the staff to his collar to the roll’s edge and back, an old man’s hand hunting a place to land and finding none.

“I read forty-three names at dawn,” Tomas said. “Nothing stopped me. I would have noticed a stranger.” He closed the notebook without squaring it against anything. “Whoever it was — I knew them.”

Elias’s eyes went up then, over their heads, to the black walls where the carved names climbed out of lamplight into dark, thousands upon thousands, the living and the dead and the departed and the returned — and Lyra watched him do what she had done that morning: search the stone for an absence with no idea of its shape.

The eldest clerk drew a line beneath the roll and dated it. If her hand was unsteady, she was old; hands are.

People left in ones and twos, quietly, the way people leave a sickroom. No one argued. There was nothing to argue with.

At the doors, Lyra stopped beneath the wall and let her eyes climb it. That morning she had stood in this spot with the unreasonable feeling that one name was missing, and had scolded herself for it.

The feeling had been reasonable after all.

It had only been early.

Word had gone ahead of them, the way word does. The market was loud and gentle at once. Shopkeepers called the Pilgrims by name — and Lyra heard the small catch in it now, each name checked against something as it was said. A baker pressed warm rolls into Tomas’s hands despite his protests. A florist tucked a white blossom behind Lyra’s ear.

An elderly man she had never met clasped her shoulders. “Carry us well,” he said, and was gone into the crowd before she could ask what he meant.

⸻

That afternoon they climbed the western wall overlooking Orin. It had become their place years ago. From up there they could see nearly the whole city — the river, the market, the towers. Beyond everything else—

The mountain.

Clouds wrapped its summit. Not thick enough to hide it. Only enough to suggest it preferred a little privacy.

Tomas spread a page across the stones — every recorded Pilgrimage leader for nearly two centuries — and weighted its corners with four pebbles, which he then evened.

“You brought diagrams.”

“I always bring diagrams.”

“You have entirely too much free time.”

“I use it efficiently.”

But his attention kept leaving the page. It went to the satchel where the notebook lay; it came back. He had not opened the book since the hall.

“You know,” Lyra said, “one day you’ll discover the Pilgrimage is exactly what everyone says it is.”

“I consider that statistically unlikely.”

“What if it’s simply a journey?”

“It isn’t.”

“How do you know?”

“Because nobody returns willing to describe the ending.”

She thought of every adult she had ever asked. Master Rian had described his boots. Mara had described being frightened. The baker had once spent a quarter hour on which breads traveled well. Not one of them had ever described arriving. Whenever a conversation approached the summit it drifted elsewhere — not abruptly, not on purpose — like rivers flowing around a stone.

“What if they can’t explain it?” she asked.

Tomas’s gaze went from the diagram to the mountain and stayed. “That,” he admitted, “is possible.”

It was the closest thing to surrender she had ever heard from him.

Below, the lamplighters started on the market road. Lyra pulled her knees up and asked it at the city, not at him. “Whose name was it?”

For a while he aligned the pebbles that were already aligned.

“Unestablished,” he said — and for the first time since she had known him, the word meant what other people meant by it.

Shadows lengthened. Families lit their lanterns; smoke rose from hundreds of chimneys; the streets went gold under the last of the sun.

“It’s beautiful,” Lyra said.

“It always has been.”

“I’ve never noticed.”

“You’ve never had reason to think you were leaving.”

“I’m coming back.”

“I know.” He said it at once, without the pause every adult kept in that place.

She smiled. “Thank you.”

“What for?”

“For saying it like you believe it.”

“I do believe it.”

Then, from the eastern tower, the evening bells began.

Tomas took out the compass and set it on the warm stone between them, turning it until the dial’s north faced north. Through the first bell the needle wandered, lazy as ever. Through the second. Through the third. On the last it drifted — unhurried, as though it had all the time in the world — until it pointed at the mountain.

It stayed there, trembling, for as long as the final note hung in the air.

Then the note faded, and the needle let go.

Neither of them said anything. Tomas put the compass away with both hands.

Far below, Elias crossed the square alone. He stopped. Faced the mountain. Then he glanced beside himself, the way you glance at someone to share a thought too small for words.

No one was there.

He stood a moment longer, then went on toward the Hall of Names.

The last echo of the bells hung over Orin, thinning. Lyra caught herself listening for another note. A note where none belonged. There wasn’t one. Not this time. She wasn’t sure which she had wanted.

In seven days, forty-two Pilgrims would climb the mountain. Everyone said so. The roll said so. Tomas’s notebook said so, twice, in Tomas’s handwriting.

All the way home, Lyra counted forty-three.
