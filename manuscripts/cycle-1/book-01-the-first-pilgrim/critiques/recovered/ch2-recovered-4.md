## Chapter 2 — Tomas Knows Everything

The Hall of Names smelled of stone dust and candlewax. People spoke quietly here — not because anyone asked them to, but because every voice seemed to linger among the carved names, as though the walls remembered conversations long after the speakers had forgotten them.

Lyra stood where Elias had left her. The old caretaker had disappeared into the crowd as quickly as he had unsettled her.

Tomas was counting.

He stood at the board beside the doors, where the year's roll hung: the Pilgrims of their year in a clerk's fair hand, one name under another like rungs. His finger moved down the column. At the bottom it returned to the top. Tomas never counted anything twice unless the world had made a mistake.

Lyra crossed to him, past two mothers holding one conversation in two whispers.

"Count these," Tomas said. He had not turned around.

"Good morning to you as well."

"Count them."

She set her finger where his had been and counted, name by name, ink and paper-grain under her fingertip, to the bottom of the column.

"Forty-three."

"Again."

"You've counted them yourself."

"Three times. The number is not embarrassed by repetition."

She counted again, slower. Forty-three names — and she knew every one of them. That was the part she noticed second. Names she had raced against, sat next to at winter suppers. She read the column a third time hunting the stranger, and the column did the thing the arithmetic said it couldn't.

Every name belonged.

Her own name sat third from the top. She had never been third at anything. Someone had changed the order and told no one why; on an ordinary day that would have occupied Tomas until supper. Today he had spent one glance on the leader's paper, pinned beside the roll — "Seren," in the tone of a man collecting a debt. "No longer adjacent to the truth. Merely the truth." — and gone back to the column.

"There are forty-two Pilgrims this year," he said. "I have documented our year since we were six, and we are forty-two." He opened his notebook and held it flat. There, in handwriting small enough to hide from the world, sat the morning's entry — the roll, counted at dawn before the doors opened — a number boxed in with four straight strokes, the way he boxed anything he intended to keep.

Forty-three.

"You counted this at dawn," Lyra said, "and mentioned nothing at breakfast."

"At breakfast it was a clerical error. Clerical errors are not conversation."

"And now?"

"Now I have counted five times, you have counted twice, and one of us is wrong by a person."

"Then read me the stranger."

"I have tried. I read for a stranger twice this morning. Every name I checked belonged to a Pilgrim I know." He tapped the board's frame, once. "I checked forty-two names. There were still forty-three."

Her thumb had found the bird through her coat pocket without her asking it to. She made it stop. "Ask Elias. He knows everyone."

"I intend to. He is mystery four." Tomas counted the others off on his fingers. "He recognized the bird. He corrected himself almost immediately. Whatever frightened him wasn't the bird." The fourth finger he pointed at the roll. "And the Hall of Names cannot add."

"What did frighten him, then?"

"Unestablished."

"Most people would say 'I don't know.'"

"They are not the same sentence. One is a hole. The other is a hole I intend to fill."

"It's the rarest sentence in the Tomas language."

"It is not rare." He closed the notebook. "It is extinct."

Lyra turned back to the column. "One day something will make you say it."

"When it does, I'll write it down."

At the front of the hall, the eldest of the clerks rang a small hand-bell. Lines began to form. Registration would take the morning; the reading of the roll would close it at midday, every name read aloud and answered.

Tomas wrote the count a second time beneath the first, boxed it, and turned the book toward her. "Read it back."

"Forty-three."

"Again."

"Forty-three, Tomas."

"Good." He shut the notebook. "A number two people have read is harder to lose."

"You're not telling the clerks?"

"After the reading. If forty-three answer, the count of Pilgrims is wrong. If forty-two answer, the roll is."

Registration was three clerks at a long table, confirming names, family records, and the small collection of belongings each traveler intended to carry.

"No weapons beyond a walking knife," the youngest clerk recited, for what sounded like the hundredth time. "No written histories. No family heirlooms that cannot be replaced. No animals."

The rules had remained unchanged for generations. No one seemed to know why.

"Lyra Ash." The eldest clerk smiled. She confirmed Lyra's age, her household, her health, and after each answer the charcoal stylus made its small dry sound against the page.

"And what will you carry?"

She named it: food, a blanket, a rain cloak, a cup, a knife, spare stockings. Six ticks of the stylus. Six replaceable things.

"Anything else?"

The bird had been in her coat pocket since morning. She had not put it there — or she had, and the putting had left no mark on her, as sleep leaves none. She could feel it against her ribs when she breathed.

Behind her in the line came a fainter scratching — Tomas, documenting procedure while he waited. The clerks permitted him the way you permit weather. Six declared. She felt his glance land on her pocket the way you feel sun through wool.

Her thumb found the chipped wing.

"Nothing that can't be replaced."

The rule's own words, handed back. All her life she had turned other people's sentences over, feeling for the seam. Now, needing one, she had borrowed theirs.

The stylus scratched. The fainter scratching behind her had stopped.

She did not turn her head.

Then the clerk set the stylus aside and looked up — at her, not at the record of her. "You understand that the Pilgrimage is voluntary."

Every Pilgrim received the same question. Everyone already knew the answer. Still, the clerk waited — properly waited, hands folded — as though the question were being asked for the first time in the history of the world.

"I understand."

"And you choose to make the ascent of your own free will?"

"I do."

It was true. She said it the way you set a true thing down next to a false one: carefully, without letting them touch.

The clerk slid a narrow silver thread across the table. "Then welcome."

Lyra took the thread and turned. Tomas's gaze came up from her pocket to her face and held there, one beat, two. Six declared; seven carried; Tomas kept accounts the way other people kept promises. She watched him not do it — not open the notebook, not raise the count, not say the seventh thing into the record. Then he stepped past her to the table and gave the clerks his name.

It should have felt like kindness. It weighed more than the bird.

On the Hall steps she tied the thread around her wrist. It weighed nothing. She was aware of it for the rest of the day.

And she wondered, for the first time in her life, what would happen if a Pilgrim said no. She could not decide which was stranger: that the question was always asked, or that it had never once been needed.

Under the maple tree that shaded the steps, she waited for Tomas. He came out at last with his satchel straps squared and his mouth set.

"How long did they question you?"

"Eight minutes."

"They questioned me for one."

"You presumably failed to provide context," he said, thumb working the strap-seam.

"You always provide context."

"They should appreciate thoroughness."

"They do not," she said, watching his face.

He sighed. "They rarely do."

Eight minutes of context, and not one word about her pocket. She did not thank him. There was no way to thank him that was not a confession.

"Lyra."

Elias stood in the doorway. Up close he seemed older than he had across the hall — lines crossed his face like rivers on an old map.

"I owe you an apology," he said.

"For what?"

"I startled you."

"A little."

He nodded. "I've been… remembering poorly today."

Lyra waited for the explanation the sentence deserved. None came.

Instead his glance dropped to her coat pocket, then rose and held her eyes. "May I?"

She gave him the bird. He turned it over with care, and his thumb paused at the chipped wing.

"It has been well loved."

"My father made it."

"So you said."

"You've seen it before." She leaned in. "Where?"

"I have seen many birds." He turned it once more, slower, and his shoulders came down half an inch. "He did good work." He handed it back. "Take good care of it."

"I always have."

"I know."

Then he went still, like a man stopped halfway through a sentence.

Tomas had given up pretending not to watch. "Elias. Did you know my mother?"

"Sel. Of course."

"You remembered her quickly."

"I remember many things quickly."

"And slowly?"

The smile faded enough to notice. "Those are the troublesome ones."

Tomas brought out an old brass compass, its glass cracked, its needle wandering instead of settling. "It was my mother's."

Elias took it with both hands, the way you take something that is still warm. "She carried this."

"Every journey."

"So she did." He tapped the glass once. The needle trembled — and for a moment it pointed, not north, but at the mountain. Then it wandered again.

"Strange," Elias murmured.

"It hasn't worked since she died."

"Perhaps it still does." He handed it back. "But not in the way it was made to."

Tomas opened his mouth, closed it, and filed nothing.

From the doorway the hand-bell rang twice — the reading of the roll. Elias excused himself with a small bow, and they followed him in.

The hall had filled again. Forty-two Pilgrims stood in loose rows with their families behind them, Elias beneath the central arch, and the eldest clerk stood with the roll in her hands, unpinned from its board.

She read the way clerks read, a name and a breath and a name.

"Anet."

"Here."

"Cor."

"Here."

Name after name went up, and each came back answered — one boy answering twice in case the first had not counted. The reading found a rhythm, and the rhythm was the pleasant kind, the kind that belonged to ovens and looms and morning bells. Lyra counted without deciding to.

"Forty-two names," the clerk said at last, folding the roll. "The roll is read. The—"

"Forty-three," Tomas said.

The word carried the way dropped metal carries.

The eldest clerk found him in the second row. "The roll holds forty-two names."

"At dawn it held forty-three." Tomas came forward, notebook open. "I counted before the doors opened. She counted after me." He tipped his head toward Lyra.

The clerk's eyes moved to her.

"With my hand," Lyra said. "Finger under every name."

"Then you miscounted, young man."

"I have never miscounted." He said it the way other people said their own names.

The clerk unfolded the roll and went down it with her finger, lips moving, while the hall held its breath around her.

"Forty-two."

"Aloud," said a gray-haired woman near the front. Something in her voice made the clerk obey.

She began at the top, aloud this time, and the hall counted with her — parents and Pilgrims and children hoisted onto shoulders, keeping the tally in one slow voice that climbed the numbers the way Pilgrims climb the mountain, evenly, all together, no one hurrying, up through the twenties, through the thirties, past forty — and stopped, because there was nowhere else for it to go.

"Forty-two," said the hall.

The quiet did not arrive all at once. It moved through the crowd like cold through an open door.

Lyra had counted with them. Her own mouth had said it with everyone else's.

Her finger remembered otherwise.

The gray-haired woman spoke into the quiet. "I counted forty-three this morning. When I brought my son. I decided I'd counted badly, and I didn't count again." Her hands smoothed her skirt, over and over, though it lay flat. "I didn't want to."

Another woman said the reading had felt long to her — longer than forty-two names. She could not say why. She had not counted. It had used more of the morning than it should have.

A man by the doors said the extra name had been low on the page — an old family name, he thought. He got as far as the first letter. His mouth stayed open around a sound that never arrived, and he shut it, and studied his shoes as though the name had fallen there.

They began raising names then — sons and daughters, the smith's boy, the twins from the mill row — and every name anyone raised was on the roll, and had a Pilgrim under it, and had already answered for itself once that day. Every name anyone could think of was there. Whatever was gone was not among the things that could be thought of.

Tomas turned to his morning page to read the proof aloud, and stopped.

The number sat in its box, four straight strokes around it, in his own small hand.

Forty-two.

He turned back a page, forward a page. Beneath the first entry the second said the same thing. Forty-two, twice, in handwriting no one else could have imitated, because no one else could write that small.

"I wrote forty-three," he said. To the notebook, not to the hall.

He turned the page toward Lyra without being asked. She had read the number back to him twice, because a number two people have read is harder to lose. The memory was still whole — the pencil, the box, the figures — and under the memory the page said what it said. There was nothing in her hands to hold against it.

She went forward and counted the roll herself, finger touching each name. The column was seamless. No gap, no crowding, no half-made letter. The clerks did the sensible things: they counted the morning's signatures — forty-two; they counted the threads cut from the silver spool — forty-two; they held the roll to the light — if a name had gone out of it, the paper had closed over the place where it had been.

It was Lyra who turned toward the arch. "Elias." Her voice carried in that hall whether she asked it to or not. "You keep the names. Whose was the forty-third?"

The crowd made the kind of room for him it made for no one else in Orin. He stood before the roll and read it from the top, lips shaping forty-two names like a prayer he suspected of missing a verse. Then he read it again.

The hall waited. He was the caretaker of the names. He had known everyone in Orin for longer than their grandparents had been alive, and no one had ever tested it, the way no one tests a bridge they cross daily.

"I don't know," Elias said.

All her life Lyra had heard him say those words, and they had always sounded like an apology.

They did not sound like one now.

Elias did not go still. That was the worst of it. Stillness was the thing Lyra knew him by, the way she knew Mara by her busy hands — and now his hand moved at his side, searching for the staff it was already holding.

"Whoever it was," Tomas said, "I knew them."

Elias's eyes went up then, over their heads, to the black walls where the carved names climbed out of lamplight into dark, thousands upon thousands, the living and the dead and the departed and the returned — and Lyra watched him do what she had done that morning: search the stone for an absence with no idea of its shape.

He turned to the eldest clerk. "Your mother's name." She blinked, and gave it. "And her mother's?" She gave that too. He nodded once, like a man testing a rope before trusting his weight to it. The rope held. It did not seem to comfort him.

"Forgive me," he said.

He said it to the wall of names.

The eldest clerk drew a line beneath the roll and dated it. If her hand was unsteady, she was old; hands are.

The crowd did not disperse so much as change the subject. There was dough rising in a hundred kitchens, there were children announcing themselves hungry, and by ones and twos the morning let itself be ordinary again. By the doors a woman counted her children as they reached her. Three. Lyra saw her count them again.

Tomas stayed at the board and copied the roll entire — all forty-two names into the notebook, in handwriting larger than any she had ever seen him use, pressing hard enough to leave the words standing in the next three pages.

At the doors, Lyra stopped beneath the wall and let her eyes climb it. That morning she had stood in this spot with the unreasonable feeling that one name was missing, and had scolded herself for it.

The feeling had been reasonable after all.

It had only been early.

Word had outrun them into the market. The streets were loud and gentle at once: knots of neighbors loosened as Pilgrims passed and retied behind them. Shopkeepers called the Pilgrims by name — and Lyra heard the small catch in it now, each name checked against something as it was said. A baker pressed warm rolls into Tomas's hands despite his protests. A florist tucked another white blossom behind Lyra's ear.

An elderly man she had never met clasped her shoulders. "Carry us well," he said, and was gone into the crowd before she could ask what he meant.

That afternoon they climbed the western wall overlooking Orin. It had become their place years ago. From up there they could see nearly the whole city — the river, the market, the towers. Beyond everything else—

The mountain.

Clouds wrapped its summit. Not thick enough to hide it. Only enough to suggest it preferred a little privacy.

Tomas sat with the satchel between his boots, flap buckled. No diagrams. No lists. In all their years on the wall she had never once sat beside him without paper between them.

"You're not going to chart it," she said.

"Every count I have ever made is now provisional. I am deciding what follows from that."

Lyra spread her hand flat on the stone. "Say the count was right at dawn. Then somewhere in Orin there's an empty bed. A coat with nobody in it. A bowl too many at someone's table."

"Or nothing is missing, and the record was wrong."

"Do you believe that?"

His hand went to the satchel buckle and came away. "The inventory is short. I can prove the shortage. I cannot produce the item."

"You know," she said, "one day you'll discover the Pilgrimage is exactly what everyone says it is."

"I consider that statistically unlikely."

"What if it's simply a journey?"

"It isn't."

"How do you know?"

"Because nobody returns willing to describe the ending."

She thought of every adult she had ever asked. Master Rian had described his boots. Mara had described being frightened. The baker had once spent a quarter hour on which breads traveled well. Not one of them had ever described arriving. Whenever a conversation approached the summit it drifted elsewhere — not abruptly, not on purpose — like rivers flowing around a stone.

"What if they can't explain it?" she asked.

Tomas's gaze went from the buckled satchel to the mountain and stayed. "That," he admitted, "is possible."

It was the closest thing to surrender she had ever heard from him.

Below them the lamplighters started on the market road. "Whose name was it?" She asked it at the city, not at him.

He watched the lamps come on, one by one.

"Unestablished," he said — and for the first time since she had known him, the word meant what other people meant by it.

"What will you carry?" he asked.

"You heard the list."

"I heard it." He watched the mountain, not her. "Six things."

The number sat between them. It was not a large number. Something was missing from it, and each of them knew that the other could count.

"Six things," she said again.

His eyes went to her coat pocket, held there the length of a breath, and came back to the mountain. He did not open the notebook. It was the first thing she had ever known him to leave uncounted.

Shadows lengthened. Families lit their lanterns; smoke rose from hundreds of chimneys; the streets went gold under the last of the sun.

"It's beautiful," Lyra said.

"It always has been."

"I've never noticed."

"You've never had reason to think you were leaving."

"I'm coming back."

"I know." He said it at once, without the pause every adult kept in that place.

She smiled. "Thank you."

"What for?"

"For saying it like you believe it."

"I do believe it."

Then, from the eastern tower, the evening bells began.

Tomas brought out the compass. Lyra held out her hand, and he gave it to her. It sat in her palm with its honest weight; whatever had happened to the roll had not happened to it. She handed it back.

He set it on the warm stone between them, turning it until the dial's north faced north. Through the first bell the needle wandered, lazy as ever. Through the second. Through the third. On the last it drifted — unhurried, as though it had all the time in the world — until it pointed at the mountain.

It stayed there, trembling, for as long as the final note hung in the air.

Then the note faded, and the needle let go.

Neither of them said anything. Tomas put the compass away with both hands.

Far below, Elias crossed the square alone. He stopped. Faced the mountain. Then he glanced beside himself, the way you glance at someone to share a thought too small for words.

No one was there.

He stood a moment longer, then went on toward the Hall of Names.

In seven days, forty-two Pilgrims would climb the mountain. The roll said so. The hall had said so, with one voice. Tomas's notebook said so, twice, in Tomas's handwriting.

All the way home, Lyra counted forty-three.
